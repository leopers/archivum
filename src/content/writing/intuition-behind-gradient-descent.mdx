Optimization is often introduced as an instruction: compute the gradient, step in the opposite direction, repeat. But the instruction hides a choice of geometry.

## The local picture

For a differentiable function $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, a first-order approximation near $x$ is

$$f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top \Delta x.$$

Among all steps with fixed Euclidean length, the change is most negative when $\Delta x$ points against $\nabla f(x)$. This gives the familiar update

$$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta \nabla f(x_t).$$

The word _Euclidean_ matters. Change the notion of distance and the steepest direction changes too. Natural gradient methods and preconditioning are, in part, ways of choosing a more useful geometry.

## A small implementation

```python
def gradient_descent(grad, x, step=0.05, iterations=500):
    history = [x]
    for _ in range(iterations):
        x = x - step * grad(x)
        history.append(x)
    return x, history
```

> The gradient is not intrinsically a direction. A metric turns the differential into one.

This viewpoint makes ill-conditioning less mysterious: narrow valleys are places where our default ruler is badly matched to the landscape.

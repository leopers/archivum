## Motivation

In many undergraduate ODE courses taught in Brazil, existence and uniqueness are presented more as facts than as results to be understood. Picard’s method of successive approximations is introduced, a sequence of inequalities is given, and convergence to a unique solution is asserted. The mechanism connecting these steps often remains unclear.

This was how I first encountered the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem. Picard’s method seemed like a construction specific to differential equations, and the reason it established both existence and uniqueness was hidden inside the calculations.

In France, I learned a different presentation. One first proves Banach’s fixed-point theorem. The Cauchy problem is then rewritten as an integral equation, which defines a map on a complete space of functions. Its fixed point is precisely a solution of the ODE.

From this viewpoint, Picard’s method is the method of successive approximations applied to a contracting map. This article develops that argument from the metric-space foundations to the local Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem.

## The initial-value problem

Let $I\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be an interval, let $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be open, and consider a function

$$
f:I\times U\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^n.
$$

Given an initial time $t_0\in I$ and an initial state $x_0\in U$, we want to study the problem

$$
\begin{cases}
\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t)),\\
x(t_0)=x_0.
\end{cases}
$$

This is an **initial-value problem**, also called a **Cauchy problem**. Let $J\subseteq I$ be an interval containing $t_0$. A function

$$
x:J\longrightarrow U
$$

is a solution on $J$ if $x\in C^1(J,U)$ and

$$
x(t_0)=x_0,
\qquad
\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t))
\quad\text{for every }t\in J.
$$

The interval $J$ is part of the solution. We initially ask two distinct questions.

**Existence.** Is there an interval $J$ containing $t_0$ on which at least one solution is defined?

**Uniqueness.** If $x_1$ and $x_2$ are two solutions with the same initial condition, must they agree wherever they are both defined near $t_0$?

The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem gives conditions on $f$ that answer both questions locally, on an interval around $t_0$.

Notice that neither question asks us to find a closed formula for the solution. Before solving an equation explicitly, we want to know whether there is in fact a well-defined object to find. This is the point at which the problem leaves elementary calculation and becomes a question in analysis.

### From the differential equation to an integral equation

**Lemma.** Suppose that $f$ is continuous. A continuous function $x:J\to U$ solves the Cauchy problem if and only if

$$
x(t)=x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds
\qquad\text{for every }t\in J.
$$

**Proof.** If $x$ is a solution, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

$$
x(t)-x(t_0)
=
\int_{t_0}^{t}\dot{x}(s)\,ds
=
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds.
$$

The initial condition gives the integral formula. Conversely, if the formula holds, then

$$
t\longmapsto\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds
$$

is continuously differentiable. Differentiating recovers

$$
\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t)),
$$

and evaluation at $t=t_0$ gives $x(t_0)=x_0$.

This equivalence is the decisive change of viewpoint. Differentiation is replaced by integration, and the unknown curve now appears as the input of a map acting on functions.

The integral formulation reveals the fixed-point structure. Define the Picard map $\Phi$ by

$$
\Phi(y)(t)
=
x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,y(s))\,ds.
$$

Solving the initial-value problem is therefore equivalent to finding $x$ such that

$$
\Phi(x)=x.
$$

It remains to choose a complete function space on which $\Phi$ is contracting.

## Metric and complete spaces

The map $\Phi$ acts on functions. To speak meaningfully about successive approximations, we need to measure the distance between two functions. We also need to know that, if these approximations approach one another, their limit is still an admissible function. Metric spaces and completeness formalize these two requirements.

### Distances and metric spaces

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a set and let

$$
d:X\times X\longrightarrow[0,+\infty)
$$

be a map. We say that $d$ is a **distance** on $X$ if, for all $x,y,z\in X$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x,y)=0 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=y &&\text{(separation)},\\
d(x,y)&=d(y,x) &&\text{(symmetry)},\\
d(x,z)&\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z) &&\text{(triangle inequality)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The pair $(X,d)$ is then called a **metric space**.

**Definition.** A sequence $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ in $X$ converges to $x\in X$ if

$$
d(x_n,x)\longrightarrow 0
\qquad\text{as }n\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

It is a **Cauchy sequence** if

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0,\quad
\exists n_0\in\mathbb{N},\quad
\forall n,m\geq n_0,\quad
d(x_n,x_m)<\varepsilon.
$$

**Proposition.** Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence.

**Proof.** Suppose that $x_n\to x$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $n_0\in\mathbb{N}$ such that

$$
n\geq n_0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
d(x_n,x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$

Hence, for all $n,m\geq n_0$, the triangle inequality gives

$$
d(x_n,x_m)
\leq d(x_n,x)+d(x,x_m)
<\varepsilon.
$$

The converse need not hold.

This distinction is important because the method of successive approximations first gives information about the distance between two terms of a sequence. It does not provide the limit in advance. The Cauchy property is therefore the natural intermediate step in the fixed-point proof.

### Complete metric spaces

**Definition.** A metric space $(X,d)$ is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges in $X$.

The limit must belong to the space. For example, $\mathbb{Q}$ with the usual distance is not complete: a Cauchy sequence of rational numbers may converge in $\mathbb{R}$ to an irrational number.

Completeness rules out precisely this loss of the limit. Later, once the successive approximations are shown to form a Cauchy sequence, completeness will turn that asymptotic information into the existence of a point in the space.

### The Banach space of continuous functions

Let

$$
J=[t_0-a,t_0+a]
$$

be a compact interval. We denote by

$$
C(J;\mathbb{R}^n)
$$

the vector space of continuous functions from $J$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$. On this space, consider the norm of uniform convergence

$$
\lVert y\rVert_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in J}|y(t)|.
$$

The associated distance is

$$
d_\infty(y,z)
=
\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in J}|y(t)-z(t)|.
$$

A complete normed vector space is a **Banach space**.

For the Cauchy problem, the relevant space is not merely $\mathbb{R}^n$ but a space of continuous curves. The norm of uniform convergence is the appropriate choice because it controls two curves simultaneously at every time in $J$.

**Theorem.** The space

$$
\bigl(C(J;\mathbb{R}^n),\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty\bigr)
$$

is a Banach space.

**Proof.** Let $(y_n)_{n\geq 0}$ be a Cauchy sequence for $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$. For every $t\in J$ and $n,m\geq 0$,

$$
|y_n(t)-y_m(t)|
\leq
\lVert y_n-y_m\rVert_\infty.
$$

Thus $(y_n(t))_{n\geq 0}$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{R}^n$, so we may define

$$
y(t)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}y_n(t).
$$

Given $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $n_0\in\mathbb{N}$ such that, for all $n,m\geq n_0$,

$$
\lVert y_n-y_m\rVert_\infty<\varepsilon.
$$

Letting $m\to+\infty$ yields, for every $n\geq n_0$,

$$
\lVert y_n-y\rVert_\infty\leq\varepsilon.
$$

Thus $y_n\to y$ uniformly on $J$. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, so $y\in C(J;\mathbb{R}^n)$. Therefore the space is complete.

We have now identified a setting in which a convergent construction cannot leave the space of continuous functions. The remaining ingredient is a map that forces successive approximations to approach one another.

## Contractions and Banach’s fixed-point theorem

### Contracting maps

**Definition.** Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space. A map

$$
f:X\longrightarrow X
$$

is said to be **contracting** if there exists $k\in[0,1)$ such that, for all $x,y\in X$,

$$
d\bigl(f(x),f(y)\bigr)
\leq
k\,d(x,y).
$$

Equivalently, $f$ is $k$-Lipschitz with $k<1$.

The condition $k<1$ is what distinguishes a contraction from an arbitrary Lipschitz map: every application reduces distances by a uniform factor. Repeating the map therefore produces geometric decay.

In particular, every contracting map is continuous. Indeed, if $x_n\to x$, then

$$
d\bigl(f(x_n),f(x)\bigr)
\leq
k\,d(x_n,x)
\longrightarrow 0.
$$

A point $x\in X$ is a **fixed point** of $f$ if

$$
f(x)=x.
$$

### Banach’s fixed-point theorem

**Theorem.** Let $(X,d)$ be a nonempty complete metric space and let $f:X\to X$ be a contracting map. Then $f$ has a unique fixed point in $X$.

The theorem is constructive. It does not assume that the fixed point is already known; it produces it from an arbitrary starting point.

### Proof: existence by successive approximations

Choose any $x_0\in X$ and define the sequence $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ by

$$
x_{n+1}=f(x_n),
\qquad n\geq 0.
$$

Successive applications of the contraction property give, for every $n\geq 0$,

$$
d(x_{n+1},x_n)
\leq
k^n d(x_1,x_0).
$$

Let $m>n$. By the triangle inequality,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x_m,x_n)
&\leq
\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}d(x_{j+1},x_j)\\
&\leq
\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}k^j d(x_1,x_0)\\
&\leq
\frac{k^n}{1-k}d(x_1,x_0).
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $k\in[0,1)$,

$$
\frac{k^n}{1-k}d(x_1,x_0)
\longrightarrow 0
\qquad\text{as }n\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

Therefore $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ is a Cauchy sequence. Since $X$ is complete, there exists $x_\infty\in X$ such that

$$
x_n\longrightarrow x_\infty.
$$

The map $f$ is continuous, and hence

$$
f(x_\infty)
=
f\left(\lim_{n\to+\infty}x_n\right)
=
\lim_{n\to+\infty}f(x_n)
=
\lim_{n\to+\infty}x_{n+1}
=
x_\infty.
$$

Thus $x_\infty$ is a fixed point of $f$.

### Error bound

The preceding proof also controls the error after a finite number of successive approximations. Letting $m\to+\infty$ in the inequality above gives

$$
d(x_\infty,x_n)
\leq
\frac{k^n}{1-k}d(x_1,x_0).
$$

This bounds the error after $n$ successive approximations.

### Proof of uniqueness

Suppose that $x,y\in X$ are fixed points of $f$. Then

$$
d(x,y)
=
d\bigl(f(x),f(y)\bigr)
\leq
k\,d(x,y).
$$

Since $k<1$, necessarily $d(x,y)=0$, hence $x=y$.

The proof contains the complete mechanism we need for the ODE. Contraction makes the successive approximations Cauchy, completeness supplies their limit, and continuity identifies that limit as a fixed point. The contraction inequality then gives uniqueness.

## A precise local existence and uniqueness theorem

We can now translate this abstract argument back to differential equations. We first prove a precise form of the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem whose hypotheses allow a direct application of Banach’s theorem.

Let $\delta>0$ and $(t_0,x_0)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n$. Define

$$
A=
\left\{
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n:
|t-t_0|\leq\delta,
\ |x-x_0|\leq\delta
\right\}.
$$

Suppose that $f:A\to\mathbb{R}^n$ is continuous and that there exist $M,C>0$ such that

$$
|f(t,x)|\leq M
\qquad
\text{for every }(t,x)\in A,
$$

and

$$
|f(t,x)-f(t,y)|
\leq
C|x-y|
$$

whenever $(t,x),(t,y)\in A$.

The constant $M$ controls how far a solution can move during a given time, while $C$ controls how differently two curves are transformed by the vector field. These two bounds determine the interval on which the fixed-point construction works.

Set

$$
T=
\min\left\{
\delta,
\frac{\delta}{2M},
\frac{1}{2C}
\right\}
$$

and

$$
J=[t_0-T,t_0+T].
$$

The three terms in the definition of $T$ have distinct roles. The condition $T\leq\delta$ keeps time inside $A$; $MT\leq\delta/2$ keeps the curve inside the spatial ball; and $CT\leq1/2$ makes the Picard map contracting.

**Theorem.** Under the preceding hypotheses, there exists a unique function $x\in C^1(J;\mathbb{R}^n)$ satisfying

$$
\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t)),
\qquad
x(t_0)=x_0,
$$

and

$$
(t,x(t))\in A
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in J.
$$

### The complete metric space

Let

$$
\overline{B}(x_0,\delta)
=
\left\{
x\in\mathbb{R}^n:
|x-x_0|\leq\delta
\right\}
$$

and define

$$
E=C\bigl(J;\overline{B}(x_0,\delta)\bigr).
$$

Restricting the image of the curves is essential. The function $f$ is only controlled on $A$, so the Picard map must not be allowed to evaluate $f$ outside this set.

Equip $E$ with the distance

$$
d_\infty(y,z)
=
\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in J}|y(t)-z(t)|.
$$

The constant function $t\mapsto x_0$ belongs to $E$, so $E$ is nonempty. Moreover, $\overline{B}(x_0,\delta)$ is a closed subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and is therefore complete. Since $J$ is compact, $(E,d_\infty)$ is complete.

### The Picard map

For $y\in E$, define

$$
\Phi(y)(t)
=
x_0+
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,y(s))\,ds.
$$

The function $\Phi(y)$ is continuous. For every $t\in J$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\Phi(y)(t)-x_0|
&=
\left|
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,y(s))\,ds
\right|\\
&\leq
\left|
\int_{t_0}^{t}|f(s,y(s))|\,ds
\right|\\
&\leq
M|t-t_0|\\
&\leq
MT\\
&\leq
\frac{\delta}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $\Phi(y)\in E$, so

$$
\Phi:E\longrightarrow E.
$$

### The Picard map is contracting

Let $y,z\in E$. For every $t\in J$, the Lipschitz hypothesis on $f$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\Phi(y)(t)-\Phi(z)(t)|
&=
\left|
\int_{t_0}^{t}
\bigl(f(s,y(s))-f(s,z(s))\bigr)\,ds
\right|\\
&\leq
C\left|
\int_{t_0}^{t}|y(s)-z(s)|\,ds
\right|\\
&\leq
CT\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty\\
&\leq
\frac{1}{2}\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the supremum over $t\in J$, we obtain

$$
\lVert\Phi(y)-\Phi(z)\rVert_\infty
\leq
\frac{1}{2}\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty.
$$

Thus $\Phi$ is a contracting map on the nonempty complete metric space $(E,d_\infty)$.

All hypotheses of Banach’s theorem have now been verified. The abstract fixed point will become the desired solution through the integral formulation.

### Conclusion of the proof

Banach’s fixed-point theorem gives a unique $x\in E$ such that

$$
\Phi(x)=x.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
x(t)
=
x_0+
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in J.
$$

By the integral formulation lemma, $x\in C^1(J;\mathbb{R}^n)$ solves the Cauchy problem. The uniqueness of the fixed point gives uniqueness among solutions whose graphs remain in $A$ on $J$.

Picard’s successive approximations are therefore not an additional argument appended to the theorem. They are exactly the successive approximations in Banach’s proof, now performed in the function space $E$.

## The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem for a $C^1$ vector field

The precise theorem makes every estimate visible, but in most applications one starts from a regularity assumption on $f$ rather than from explicit constants. A $C^1$ vector field supplies the required bounds locally.

**Corollary.** Let $I\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ and $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be open, and let

$$
f:I\times U\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^n
$$

be of class $C^1$. For every $(t_0,x_0)\in I\times U$, there exist $T>0$ and a solution

$$
x:[t_0-T,t_0+T]\longrightarrow U
$$

of

$$
\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t)),
\qquad
x(t_0)=x_0.
$$

Moreover, this solution is locally unique: if $\widetilde J\subseteq[t_0-T,t_0+T]$ is an interval containing $t_0$ and $\widetilde x:\widetilde J\to U$ is another solution with $\widetilde x(t_0)=x_0$, then

$$
\widetilde x(t)=x(t)
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in\widetilde J.
$$

### Proof of existence

Since $I\times U$ is open and $(t_0,x_0)\in I\times U$, there exists $\delta>0$ such that

$$
A=
\left\{
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n:
|t-t_0|\leq\delta,
\ |x-x_0|\leq\delta
\right\}
$$

is contained in $I\times U$. The set $A$ is compact.

Because $f$ is continuous, it is bounded on $A$. Thus there exists $M>0$ such that

$$
|f(t,x)|\leq M
\qquad
\text{for every }(t,x)\in A.
$$

Let $\mathrm J_f(t,x)$ denote the Jacobian matrix of $f$ with respect to $x$. For a matrix $L$, we use the norm induced by the Euclidean norm,

$$
\lVert L\rVert
=
\sup_{|v|=1}|Lv|.
$$

This is the largest factor by which $L$ can stretch a vector. Since $f$ is of class $C^1$, the map $(t,x)\mapsto\mathrm J_f(t,x)$ is continuous. It is bounded on $A$, so there exists $C>0$ such that

$$
\lVert\mathrm J_f(t,x)\rVert
\leq C
\qquad
\text{for every }(t,x)\in A.
$$

Since $\overline B(x_0,\delta)$ is convex, the mean value inequality gives, for $(t,x),(t,y)\in A$,

$$
|f(t,x)-f(t,y)|
\leq
\left(
\sup_{z\in\overline B(x_0,\delta)}
\lVert\mathrm J_f(t,z)\rVert
\right)
|x-y|
\leq
C|x-y|.
$$

The hypotheses of the precise local theorem are therefore satisfied. With

$$
T=
\min\left\{
\delta,
\frac{\delta}{2M},
\frac{1}{2C}
\right\},
$$

that theorem gives a solution on $[t_0-T,t_0+T]$.

Thus the role of the $C^1$ hypothesis is concrete: continuity bounds the vector field on a compact neighborhood, while the bounded Jacobian makes it Lipschitz with respect to the state variable.

### Proof of uniqueness

The fixed-point argument gives uniqueness among functions in

$$
E=C\bigl([t_0-T,t_0+T];\overline B(x_0,\delta)\bigr).
$$

We remove the restriction on the image by a continuation argument.

We prove the assertion to the right of $t_0$; the proof to the left is identical. It is enough to fix an arbitrary closed interval

$$
[a,b]\subseteq\widetilde J
$$

with $a\leq t_0\leq b$, and define

$$
\gamma=
\sup\left\{
t\in[t_0,b]:
\widetilde x(s)\in\overline B(x_0,\delta)
\text{ for every }s\in[t_0,t]
\right\}.
$$

Since $\widetilde x(t_0)=x_0$ and $\widetilde x$ is continuous, $\gamma$ is well defined and $\gamma>t_0$. On $[t_0,\gamma]$, both $x$ and $\widetilde x$ satisfy the same integral equation and take their values in $\overline B(x_0,\delta)$. The fixed-point uniqueness argument, applied on this shorter interval, gives

$$
\widetilde x(t)=x(t)
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in[t_0,\gamma].
$$

The construction of $x$ gives the sharper bound

$$
|x(t)-x_0|
\leq
\frac{\delta}{2}
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in[t_0-T,t_0+T].
$$

Consequently,

$$
|\widetilde x(\gamma)-x_0|
\leq
\frac{\delta}{2}.
$$

If $\gamma<b$, continuity of $\widetilde x$ would imply that $\widetilde x(t)$ remains in $\overline B(x_0,\delta)$ for times slightly larger than $\gamma$, contradicting the definition of $\gamma$. Hence $\gamma=b$, and the two solutions coincide on $[t_0,b]$. The same argument shows that they coincide on $[a,t_0]$. Since $[a,b]\subseteq\widetilde J$ was arbitrary, they coincide on all of $\widetilde J$.

Thus a $C^1$ vector field determines a unique local solution through every initial condition. The conclusion is local because the construction only uses a compact neighborhood of $(t_0,x_0)$; extending the solution beyond this neighborhood is a separate question.

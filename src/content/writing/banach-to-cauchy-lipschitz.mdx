## Motivation

In most undergraduate ODE courses taught in Brazil, existence and uniqueness are presented as facts to be accepted rather than results to be understood. After learning how to solve a few familiar classes of equations, students are introduced to Picard iteration and told that, under suitable assumptions, it converges to a unique solution. The argument is often compressed into a sequence of estimates, leaving the central question unanswered: why should this procedure establish both existence and uniqueness?

This was how I first encountered the subject. The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem seemed to depend on a clever but rather obscure construction specific to differential equations. Picard’s method produced successive approximations, but the deeper reason for their convergence—and for the uniqueness of their limit—remained hidden behind the calculations.

While studying differential equations in France, I encountered a different presentation. Instead of beginning with Picard iteration, it begins with a general result from analysis: Banach’s fixed-point theorem. An initial-value problem is rewritten as an integral equation, and this equation defines an operator on a suitable space of functions. A solution of the ODE is then precisely a fixed point of this operator.

From this perspective, Picard iteration is no longer an isolated trick. It is simply the repeated application of a contraction, and its convergence follows from the completeness of the underlying function space. Existence and uniqueness are not separate miracles: both are consequences of the same fixed-point principle.

The purpose of this article is to develop this connection carefully. We will first establish Banach’s fixed-point theorem and understand the role played by completeness and contraction. We will then construct the Picard operator associated with an initial-value problem and show how the local Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem follows from it. The goal is to replace an opaque procedure with a clear structural argument—and to make explicit the reasoning that is too often left between the lines.

## The initial-value problem

Let $I\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be an interval, let $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be open, and consider a function

$$
f:I\times U\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^n.
$$

Given an initial time $t_0\in I$ and an initial state $x_0\in U$, we want to study the problem

$$
\begin{cases}
x'(t)=f(t,x(t)),\\
x(t_0)=x_0.
\end{cases}
$$

This is called an **initial-value problem**, or a **Cauchy problem**. The function $f$ prescribes a velocity for every admissible pair $(t,x)$. A solution is a curve whose derivative agrees at each instant with the velocity prescribed by $f$.

More precisely, let $J\subseteq I$ be an interval containing $t_0$. A function

$$
x:J\longrightarrow U
$$

is a solution on $J$ if $x\in C^1(J,U)$ and

$$
x(t_0)=x_0,
\qquad
x'(t)=f(t,x(t))
\quad\text{for every }t\in J.
$$

The interval $J$ is part of the solution. At this stage, there is no reason to expect that a solution will be defined on all of $I$. It may exist only in a neighborhood of $t_0$, or it may cease to remain in $U$ after a finite time. This is why the first theorem we will prove is a **local** result.

There are two logically distinct questions.

**Existence.** Is there an interval $J$ containing $t_0$ on which at least one solution is defined?

**Uniqueness.** If $x_1$ and $x_2$ are two solutions with the same initial condition, must they agree wherever they are both defined near $t_0$?

These questions should not be confused. An equation may admit several solutions through the same point, and it may also fail to admit any solution under insufficient regularity assumptions. The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem will give conditions on $f$ that guarantee both properties simultaneously.

### From the differential equation to an integral equation

The first important step is elementary but decisive. Suppose that $x$ is a solution on $J$. Integrating the differential equation between $t_0$ and $t$, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

$$
x(t)-x(t_0)
=
\int_{t_0}^{t}x'(s)\,ds
=
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds.
$$

Using the initial condition $x(t_0)=x_0$, we obtain

$$
x(t)
=
x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds.
$$

Conversely, suppose that $f$ is continuous and that a continuous function $x:J\to U$ satisfies this integral equation. The map

$$
t\longmapsto\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds
$$

is then continuously differentiable. Differentiating the integral equation recovers

$$
x'(t)=f(t,x(t)),
$$

and evaluating it at $t=t_0$ gives $x(t_0)=x_0$. Therefore, under the continuity of $f$, the differential and integral formulations are equivalent.

This reformulation does not solve the problem by itself: the unknown function $x$ still appears inside the integral. It does, however, reveal the correct structure. If we define the Picard operator $\Phi$ on a candidate function $y$ by

$$
\Phi(y)(t)
=
x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,y(s))\,ds,
$$

then solving the initial-value problem is exactly the same as finding a function $x$ such that

$$
\Phi(x)=x.
$$

The differential equation has become a fixed-point problem. The remaining work is to choose a suitable space of functions and determine conditions under which $\Phi$ has one—and only one—fixed point.

## The space in which Picard iteration lives

The Picard operator does not act on points of $\mathbb{R}^n$. It acts on entire functions. Before asking whether repeated applications of $\Phi$ converge, we therefore need to decide what it means for two functions to be close, and what kind of space can contain their limit.

These questions are expressed through the language of metric spaces.

### Metric spaces

Let $X$ be a set. A **metric** on $X$ is a function

$$
d:X\times X\longrightarrow[0,+\infty)
$$

such that, for every $x,y,z\in X$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x,y)&=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=y,\\
d(x,y)&=d(y,x),\\
d(x,z)&\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z).
\end{aligned}
$$

The pair $(X,d)$ is called a **metric space**. The metric contains precisely the information needed to discuss convergence: a sequence $(x_k)_{k\geq 0}$ converges to $x\in X$ when

$$
d(x_k,x)\longrightarrow 0
\qquad\text{as }k\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

For Picard iteration, however, it is not enough to recognize a limit after it has been found. We need a condition that can be checked using the iterates themselves. This leads to the notion of a Cauchy sequence.

A sequence $(x_k)_{k\geq 0}$ in $X$ is a **Cauchy sequence** if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that

$$
m,n\geq N
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon.
$$

Thus, the terms eventually become arbitrarily close to one another, without any limit having to be known in advance. Every convergent sequence is Cauchy, but the converse depends on the space.

### Why completeness matters

A metric space $(X,d)$ is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges to an element of $X$.

This condition prevents a sequence from approaching an object that lies just outside the space. For example, $\mathbb{Q}$ with the usual distance is not complete: a sequence of rational numbers may converge in $\mathbb{R}$ to an irrational number. The sequence is Cauchy in $\mathbb{Q}$, but its limit is absent from $\mathbb{Q}$.

This is exactly the obstruction we must exclude. Picard iteration will produce a sequence of functions

$$
y_{k+1}=\Phi(y_k).
$$

The contraction estimate will show that $(y_k)_{k\geq 0}$ is Cauchy. Completeness is what then guarantees that these approximations converge to a function still belonging to the space on which $\Phi$ is defined. Without it, the iteration could become increasingly coherent while failing to produce an admissible solution.

### A complete space of continuous functions

Let

$$
J=[t_0-a,t_0+a]
$$

be a compact interval. The natural ambient space for the local problem is

$$
C(J,\mathbb{R}^n),
$$

the set of continuous functions from $J$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$. We measure the distance between two functions $y,z\in C(J,\mathbb{R}^n)$ using the uniform norm

$$
\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in J}\lVert y(t)-z(t)\rVert.
$$

This choice is stronger than comparing the functions at a single time: it controls their largest separation over the whole interval. It is also well suited to the Picard operator, because an integral estimate valid for every $t\in J$ immediately becomes an estimate in the uniform norm.

The space $C(J,\mathbb{R}^n)$ is complete with respect to this norm. Indeed, let $(y_k)_{k\geq 0}$ be Cauchy in $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$. For each fixed $t\in J$, the sequence $(y_k(t))_{k\geq 0}$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{R}^n$, since

$$
\lVert y_m(t)-y_n(t)\rVert
\leq
\lVert y_m-y_n\rVert_\infty.
$$

Because $\mathbb{R}^n$ is complete, there is a pointwise limit $y(t)\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Moreover, the Cauchy estimate is uniform in $t$, so $y_k\to y$ uniformly on $J$. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous; hence $y\in C(J,\mathbb{R}^n)$. Therefore,

$$
\lVert y_k-y\rVert_\infty\longrightarrow 0,
$$

which proves completeness.

We now have a space in which a convergent Picard sequence can legitimately produce a continuous function. The next question is what property of $\Phi$ forces that convergence—and makes its limit unique.

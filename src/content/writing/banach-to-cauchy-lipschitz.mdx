## Motivation

In most undergraduate ODE courses taught in Brazil, existence and uniqueness are presented as facts to be accepted rather than results to be understood. After learning how to solve a few familiar classes of equations, students are introduced to Picard's method of successive approximations and told that, under suitable assumptions, the approximations converge to a unique solution. The argument is often compressed into a sequence of inequalities, leaving the central question unanswered: why should this procedure establish both existence and uniqueness?

This was how I first encountered the subject. The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem seemed to depend on a clever but rather obscure construction specific to differential equations. Picard’s method produced successive approximations, but the deeper reason for their convergence—and for the uniqueness of their limit—remained hidden behind the calculations.

While studying differential equations in France, I encountered a different presentation. Instead of beginning directly with Picard's method, it begins with a general result from analysis: Banach’s fixed-point theorem. An initial-value problem is rewritten as an integral equation, and this equation defines a map on a suitable space of functions. A solution of the ODE is then precisely a fixed point of this map.

From this perspective, Picard's method is no longer an isolated trick. It is the method of successive approximations applied to a contraction, and its convergence follows from the completeness of the underlying function space. Existence and uniqueness are not separate miracles: both are consequences of the same fixed-point principle.

The purpose of this article is to develop this connection carefully. We will first establish Banach’s fixed-point theorem and understand the role played by completeness and contraction. We will then construct the Picard map associated with an initial-value problem and show how the local Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem follows from it. The goal is to replace an opaque procedure with a clear structural argument—and to make explicit the reasoning that is too often left between the lines.

## The initial-value problem

Let $I\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be an interval, let $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be open, and consider a function

$$
f:I\times U\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^n.
$$

Given an initial time $t_0\in I$ and an initial state $x_0\in U$, we want to study the problem

$$
\begin{cases}
x'(t)=f(t,x(t)),\\
x(t_0)=x_0.
\end{cases}
$$

This is called an **initial-value problem**, or a **Cauchy problem**. The function $f$ prescribes a velocity for every admissible pair $(t,x)$. A solution is a curve whose derivative agrees at each instant with the velocity prescribed by $f$.

More precisely, let $J\subseteq I$ be an interval containing $t_0$. A function

$$
x:J\longrightarrow U
$$

is a solution on $J$ if $x\in C^1(J,U)$ and

$$
x(t_0)=x_0,
\qquad
x'(t)=f(t,x(t))
\quad\text{for every }t\in J.
$$

The interval $J$ is part of the solution. At this stage, there is no reason to expect that a solution will be defined on all of $I$. It may exist only in a neighborhood of $t_0$, or it may cease to remain in $U$ after a finite time. This is why the first theorem we will prove is a **local** result.

There are two logically distinct questions.

**Existence.** Is there an interval $J$ containing $t_0$ on which at least one solution is defined?

**Uniqueness.** If $x_1$ and $x_2$ are two solutions with the same initial condition, must they agree wherever they are both defined near $t_0$?

These questions should not be confused. An equation may admit several solutions through the same point, and it may also fail to admit any solution under insufficient regularity assumptions. The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem will give conditions on $f$ that guarantee both properties simultaneously.

### From the differential equation to an integral equation

The first important step is elementary but decisive. Suppose that $x$ is a solution on $J$. Integrating the differential equation between $t_0$ and $t$, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

$$
x(t)-x(t_0)
=
\int_{t_0}^{t}x'(s)\,ds
=
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds.
$$

Using the initial condition $x(t_0)=x_0$, we obtain

$$
x(t)
=
x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds.
$$

Conversely, suppose that $f$ is continuous and that a continuous function $x:J\to U$ satisfies this integral equation. The map

$$
t\longmapsto\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds
$$

is then continuously differentiable. Differentiating the integral equation recovers

$$
x'(t)=f(t,x(t)),
$$

and evaluating it at $t=t_0$ gives $x(t_0)=x_0$. Therefore, under the continuity of $f$, the differential and integral formulations are equivalent.

This reformulation does not solve the problem by itself: the unknown function $x$ still appears inside the integral. It does, however, reveal the correct structure. Define the Picard map $\Phi$ on a function $y$ by

$$
\Phi(y)(t)
=
x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,y(s))\,ds,
$$

then solving the initial-value problem is exactly the same as finding a function $x$ such that

$$
\Phi(x)=x.
$$

The differential equation has become a fixed-point problem. The remaining work is to choose a suitable space of functions and determine conditions under which $\Phi$ has one—and only one—fixed point.

## Metric and complete spaces

The map $\Phi$ acts on functions, not on points of $\mathbb{R}^n$. We must therefore place these functions in a space equipped with a notion of distance.

### Distances and metric spaces

Let $X$ be a set and let

$$
d:X\times X\longrightarrow[0,+\infty)
$$

be a map. We say that $d$ is a **distance** on $X$ if, for all $x,y,z\in X$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x,y)=0 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=y &&\text{(separation)},\\
d(x,y)&=d(y,x) &&\text{(symmetry)},\\
d(x,z)&\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z) &&\text{(triangle inequality)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The pair $(X,d)$ is then called a **metric space**.

A sequence $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ in $X$ converges to $x\in X$ if

$$
d(x_n,x)\longrightarrow 0
\qquad\text{as }n\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

It is called a **Cauchy sequence** if

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0,\quad
\exists n_0\in\mathbb{N},\quad
\forall n,m\geq n_0,\quad
d(x_n,x_m)<\varepsilon.
$$

Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence. Indeed, suppose that $x_n\to x$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $n_0\in\mathbb{N}$ such that

$$
n\geq n_0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
d(x_n,x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$

Hence, for all $n,m\geq n_0$, the triangle inequality gives

$$
d(x_n,x_m)
\leq d(x_n,x)+d(x,x_m)
<\varepsilon.
$$

The converse is not true in every metric space. This leads to the notion of completeness.

### Complete metric spaces

A metric space $(X,d)$ is said to be **complete** if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges in $X$.

The final words matter: the limit must belong to the space itself. For example, $\mathbb{Q}$ with the usual distance is not complete, since a sequence of rational numbers can be Cauchy and converge in $\mathbb{R}$ to an irrational number.

Completeness will be the hypothesis that turns successive approximations into an actual fixed point. Starting from an arbitrary $x_0\in X$, Banach’s theorem considers the sequence defined by

$$
x_{n+1}=f(x_n),
\qquad n\geq 0.
$$

The proof will show that $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ is a Cauchy sequence. If $X$ is complete, it follows that this sequence converges to some $x_\infty\in X$.

### The Banach space of continuous functions

Let

$$
J=[t_0-a,t_0+a]
$$

be a compact interval. We denote by

$$
C(J;\mathbb{R}^n)
$$

the vector space of continuous functions from $J$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$. On this space, consider the norm of uniform convergence

$$
\lVert y\rVert_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in J}\lVert y(t)\rVert.
$$

The associated distance is

$$
d_\infty(y,z)
=
\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in J}\lVert y(t)-z(t)\rVert.
$$

A complete normed vector space is called a **Banach space**. The space

$$
\bigl(C(J;\mathbb{R}^n),\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty\bigr)
$$

is a Banach space.

To verify this, let $(y_n)_{n\geq 0}$ be a Cauchy sequence for the norm $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$. For every $t\in J$ and all $n,m\geq 0$,

$$
\lVert y_n(t)-y_m(t)\rVert
\leq
\lVert y_n-y_m\rVert_\infty.
$$

Thus, for every fixed $t\in J$, the sequence $(y_n(t))_{n\geq 0}$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Since $\mathbb{R}^n$ is complete, we may define

$$
y(t)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}y_n(t).
$$

Now let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $(y_n)_{n\geq 0}$ is Cauchy for $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$, there exists $n_0\in\mathbb{N}$ such that, for all $n,m\geq n_0$,

$$
\lVert y_n-y_m\rVert_\infty<\varepsilon.
$$

Letting $m\to+\infty$ yields, for every $n\geq n_0$,

$$
\lVert y_n-y\rVert_\infty\leq\varepsilon.
$$

Therefore $y_n\to y$ uniformly on $J$. Since a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, $y\in C(J;\mathbb{R}^n)$. This proves that the space is complete.

This is the space in which we will apply the method of successive approximations to the Picard map $\Phi$. The next step is to define a contraction and prove Banach’s fixed-point theorem.

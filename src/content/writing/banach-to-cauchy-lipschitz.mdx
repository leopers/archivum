## Motivation

In most undergraduate ODE courses taught in Brazil, existence and uniqueness are presented as facts to be accepted rather than results to be understood. After learning how to solve a few familiar classes of equations, students are introduced to Picard's method of successive approximations and told that, under suitable assumptions, the approximations converge to a unique solution. The argument is often compressed into a sequence of inequalities, leaving the central question unanswered: why should this procedure establish both existence and uniqueness?

This was how I first encountered the subject. The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem seemed to depend on a clever but rather obscure construction specific to differential equations. Picard’s method produced successive approximations, but the deeper reason for their convergence—and for the uniqueness of their limit—remained hidden behind the calculations.

While studying differential equations in France, I encountered a different presentation. Instead of beginning directly with Picard's method, it begins with a general result from analysis: Banach’s fixed-point theorem. An initial-value problem is rewritten as an integral equation, and this equation defines a map on a suitable space of functions. A solution of the ODE is then precisely a fixed point of this map.

From this perspective, Picard's method is no longer an isolated trick. It is the method of successive approximations applied to a contraction, and its convergence follows from the completeness of the underlying function space. Existence and uniqueness are not separate miracles: both are consequences of the same fixed-point principle.

The purpose of this article is to develop this connection carefully. We will first establish Banach’s fixed-point theorem and understand the role played by completeness and contraction. We will then construct the Picard map associated with an initial-value problem and show how the local Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem follows from it. The goal is to replace an opaque procedure with a clear structural argument—and to make explicit the reasoning that is too often left between the lines.

## The initial-value problem

Let $I\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be an interval, let $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be open, and consider a function

$$
f:I\times U\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^n.
$$

Given an initial time $t_0\in I$ and an initial state $x_0\in U$, we want to study the problem

$$
\begin{cases}
x'(t)=f(t,x(t)),\\
x(t_0)=x_0.
\end{cases}
$$

This is called an **initial-value problem**, or a **Cauchy problem**. The function $f$ prescribes a velocity for every admissible pair $(t,x)$. A solution is a curve whose derivative agrees at each instant with the velocity prescribed by $f$.

More precisely, let $J\subseteq I$ be an interval containing $t_0$. A function

$$
x:J\longrightarrow U
$$

is a solution on $J$ if $x\in C^1(J,U)$ and

$$
x(t_0)=x_0,
\qquad
x'(t)=f(t,x(t))
\quad\text{for every }t\in J.
$$

The interval $J$ is part of the solution. At this stage, there is no reason to expect that a solution will be defined on all of $I$. It may exist only in a neighborhood of $t_0$, or it may cease to remain in $U$ after a finite time. This is why the first theorem we will prove is a **local** result.

There are two logically distinct questions.

**Existence.** Is there an interval $J$ containing $t_0$ on which at least one solution is defined?

**Uniqueness.** If $x_1$ and $x_2$ are two solutions with the same initial condition, must they agree wherever they are both defined near $t_0$?

These questions should not be confused. An equation may admit several solutions through the same point, and it may also fail to admit any solution under insufficient regularity assumptions. The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem will give conditions on $f$ that guarantee both properties simultaneously.

### From the differential equation to an integral equation

The first important step is elementary but decisive. Suppose that $x$ is a solution on $J$. Integrating the differential equation between $t_0$ and $t$, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

$$
x(t)-x(t_0)
=
\int_{t_0}^{t}x'(s)\,ds
=
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds.
$$

Using the initial condition $x(t_0)=x_0$, we obtain

$$
x(t)
=
x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds.
$$

Conversely, suppose that $f$ is continuous and that a continuous function $x:J\to U$ satisfies this integral equation. The map

$$
t\longmapsto\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds
$$

is then continuously differentiable. Differentiating the integral equation recovers

$$
x'(t)=f(t,x(t)),
$$

and evaluating it at $t=t_0$ gives $x(t_0)=x_0$. Therefore, under the continuity of $f$, the differential and integral formulations are equivalent.

This reformulation does not solve the problem by itself: the unknown function $x$ still appears inside the integral. It does, however, reveal the correct structure. Define the Picard map $\Phi$ on a function $y$ by

$$
\Phi(y)(t)
=
x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,y(s))\,ds,
$$

then solving the initial-value problem is exactly the same as finding a function $x$ such that

$$
\Phi(x)=x.
$$

The differential equation has become a fixed-point problem. The remaining work is to choose a suitable space of functions and determine conditions under which $\Phi$ has one—and only one—fixed point.

## Metric and complete spaces

The map $\Phi$ acts on functions, not on points of $\mathbb{R}^n$. We must therefore place these functions in a space equipped with a notion of distance.

### Distances and metric spaces

Let $X$ be a set and let

$$
d:X\times X\longrightarrow[0,+\infty)
$$

be a map. We say that $d$ is a **distance** on $X$ if, for all $x,y,z\in X$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x,y)=0 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=y &&\text{(separation)},\\
d(x,y)&=d(y,x) &&\text{(symmetry)},\\
d(x,z)&\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z) &&\text{(triangle inequality)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The pair $(X,d)$ is then called a **metric space**.

A sequence $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ in $X$ converges to $x\in X$ if

$$
d(x_n,x)\longrightarrow 0
\qquad\text{as }n\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

It is called a **Cauchy sequence** if

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0,\quad
\exists n_0\in\mathbb{N},\quad
\forall n,m\geq n_0,\quad
d(x_n,x_m)<\varepsilon.
$$

Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence. Indeed, suppose that $x_n\to x$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $n_0\in\mathbb{N}$ such that

$$
n\geq n_0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
d(x_n,x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$

Hence, for all $n,m\geq n_0$, the triangle inequality gives

$$
d(x_n,x_m)
\leq d(x_n,x)+d(x,x_m)
<\varepsilon.
$$

The converse is not true in every metric space. This leads to the notion of completeness.

### Complete metric spaces

A metric space $(X,d)$ is said to be **complete** if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges in $X$.

The final words matter: the limit must belong to the space itself. For example, $\mathbb{Q}$ with the usual distance is not complete, since a sequence of rational numbers can be Cauchy and converge in $\mathbb{R}$ to an irrational number.

Completeness will be the hypothesis that turns successive approximations into an actual fixed point. Starting from an arbitrary $x_0\in X$, Banach’s theorem considers the sequence defined by

$$
x_{n+1}=f(x_n),
\qquad n\geq 0.
$$

The proof will show that $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ is a Cauchy sequence. If $X$ is complete, it follows that this sequence converges to some $x_\infty\in X$.

### The Banach space of continuous functions

Let

$$
J=[t_0-a,t_0+a]
$$

be a compact interval. We denote by

$$
C(J;\mathbb{R}^n)
$$

the vector space of continuous functions from $J$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$. On this space, consider the norm of uniform convergence

$$
\lVert y\rVert_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in J}\lVert y(t)\rVert.
$$

The associated distance is

$$
d_\infty(y,z)
=
\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in J}\lVert y(t)-z(t)\rVert.
$$

A complete normed vector space is called a **Banach space**. The space

$$
\bigl(C(J;\mathbb{R}^n),\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty\bigr)
$$

is a Banach space.

To verify this, let $(y_n)_{n\geq 0}$ be a Cauchy sequence for the norm $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$. For every $t\in J$ and all $n,m\geq 0$,

$$
\lVert y_n(t)-y_m(t)\rVert
\leq
\lVert y_n-y_m\rVert_\infty.
$$

Thus, for every fixed $t\in J$, the sequence $(y_n(t))_{n\geq 0}$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Since $\mathbb{R}^n$ is complete, we may define

$$
y(t)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}y_n(t).
$$

Now let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $(y_n)_{n\geq 0}$ is Cauchy for $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$, there exists $n_0\in\mathbb{N}$ such that, for all $n,m\geq n_0$,

$$
\lVert y_n-y_m\rVert_\infty<\varepsilon.
$$

Letting $m\to+\infty$ yields, for every $n\geq n_0$,

$$
\lVert y_n-y\rVert_\infty\leq\varepsilon.
$$

Therefore $y_n\to y$ uniformly on $J$. Since a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, $y\in C(J;\mathbb{R}^n)$. This proves that the space is complete.

This is the space in which we will apply the method of successive approximations to the Picard map $\Phi$. The next step is to define a contraction and prove Banach’s fixed-point theorem.

## Contractions and Banach’s fixed-point theorem

### Contracting maps

Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space. A map

$$
f:X\longrightarrow X
$$

is said to be **contracting** if there exists $k\in[0,1)$ such that, for all $x,y\in X$,

$$
d\bigl(f(x),f(y)\bigr)
\leq
k\,d(x,y).
$$

Equivalently, $f$ is Lipschitz continuous with a Lipschitz constant $k<1$. The essential point is the strict inequality: applying $f$ reduces distances by at least a fixed factor.

In particular, every contracting map is continuous. Indeed, if $x_n\to x$, then

$$
d\bigl(f(x_n),f(x)\bigr)
\leq
k\,d(x_n,x)
\longrightarrow 0.
$$

A point $x\in X$ is called a **fixed point** of $f$ if

$$
f(x)=x.
$$

We can now state the result that will provide both existence and uniqueness for the differential equation.

### Banach’s fixed-point theorem

**Theorem.** Let $(X,d)$ be a nonempty complete metric space and let $f:X\to X$ be a contracting map. Then $f$ has a unique fixed point in $X$.

The proof is constructive: starting from an arbitrary point, it defines a sequence that converges to the fixed point.

### Proof: existence by successive approximations

Choose any $x_0\in X$ and define the sequence $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ by

$$
x_{n+1}=f(x_n),
\qquad n\geq 0.
$$

Equivalently, $x_n=f^n(x_0)$. We first prove that $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ is a Cauchy sequence.

For every $n\geq 0$, successive applications of the contraction property give

$$
d(x_{n+1},x_n)
\leq
k^n d(x_1,x_0).
$$

For $n\geq 1$, this follows from

$$
d(x_{n+1},x_n)
\leq
k,d(x_n,x_{n-1})
\leq
\cdots
\leq
k^n d(x_1,x_0).
$$

Let $m>n$. By the triangle inequality,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x_m,x_n)
&\leq
\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}d(x_{j+1},x_j)\\
&\leq
\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}k^j d(x_1,x_0)\\
&\leq
\frac{k^n}{1-k}d(x_1,x_0).
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $k\in[0,1)$,

$$
\frac{k^n}{1-k}d(x_1,x_0)
\longrightarrow 0
\qquad\text{as }n\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

Therefore $(x_n)_{n\geq 0}$ is a Cauchy sequence. Since $X$ is complete, there exists $x_\infty\in X$ such that

$$
x_n\longrightarrow x_\infty.
$$

The map $f$ is continuous, and hence

$$
f(x_\infty)
=
f\left(\lim_{n\to+\infty}x_n\right)
=
\lim_{n\to+\infty}f(x_n)
=
\lim_{n\to+\infty}x_{n+1}
=
x_\infty.
$$

Thus $x_\infty$ is a fixed point of $f$.

### Error bound

The preceding proof also controls the error after a finite number of successive approximations. Letting $m\to+\infty$ in the inequality above gives

$$
d(x_\infty,x_n)
\leq
\frac{k^n}{1-k}d(x_1,x_0).
$$

Consequently, the fixed point can be approximated with arbitrary precision, and the required number of steps can be estimated before performing them.

### Proof of uniqueness

Suppose that $x,y\in X$ are fixed points of $f$. Then

$$
d(x,y)
=
d\bigl(f(x),f(y)\bigr)
\leq
k\,d(x,y).
$$

Since $k<1$, this is possible only if $d(x,y)=0$. By the separation property of the distance, $x=y$. The fixed point is therefore unique.

Banach’s theorem has now isolated the entire mechanism: the contraction makes the successive approximations a Cauchy sequence, completeness supplies their limit, continuity makes that limit a fixed point, and the same contraction inequality gives uniqueness. It remains to verify these hypotheses for the Picard map associated with the Cauchy problem.

## A precise local existence and uniqueness theorem

We now return to the Cauchy problem

$$
\begin{cases}
x'(t)=f(t,x(t)),\\
x(t_0)=x_0.
\end{cases}
$$

The first result is a technical form of the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem. Its hypotheses are chosen so that Banach’s fixed-point theorem can be applied directly on a suitable space of continuous functions.

Let $\delta>0$ and $(t_0,x_0)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n$. Define

$$
A=
\left\{
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n:
|t-t_0|\leq\delta,
\ \lVert x-x_0\rVert\leq\delta
\right\}.
$$

Suppose that $f:A\to\mathbb{R}^n$ is continuous and that there exist constants $M>0$ and $C>0$ such that

$$
\lVert f(t,x)\rVert\leq M
\qquad
\text{for every }(t,x)\in A,
$$

and

$$
\lVert f(t,x)-f(t,y)\rVert
\leq
C\lVert x-y\rVert
$$

whenever $(t,x),(t,y)\in A$. Thus, $f$ is continuous in $(t,x)$ and Lipschitz continuous with respect to its second variable on $A$.

Set

$$
T=
\min\left\{
\delta,
\frac{\delta}{2M},
\frac{1}{2C}
\right\}
$$

and

$$
J=[t_0-T,t_0+T].
$$

**Theorem.** Under the preceding hypotheses, there exists a unique function $x\in C^1(J;\mathbb{R}^n)$ satisfying

$$
x'(t)=f(t,x(t)),
\qquad
x(t_0)=x_0,
$$

and

$$
(t,x(t))\in A
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in J.
$$

The explicit choice of $T$ is not optimal. Its purpose is to make the two estimates required by Banach’s theorem immediate: one estimate ensures that the Picard map sends the chosen function space into itself, and the other makes it contracting.

### The complete metric space

Let

$$
\overline{B}(x_0,\delta)
=
\left\{
x\in\mathbb{R}^n:
\lVert x-x_0\rVert\leq\delta
\right\}
$$

and define

$$
E=C\bigl(J;\overline{B}(x_0,\delta)\bigr).
$$

In other words, $E$ is the set of continuous functions $y:J\to\mathbb{R}^n$ whose values remain in the closed ball $\overline{B}(x_0,\delta)$. We equip $E$ with the distance

$$
d_\infty(y,z)
=
\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in J}\lVert y(t)-z(t)\rVert.
$$

The space $E$ is nonempty, since it contains the constant function $t\mapsto x_0$. The metric space $(E,d_\infty)$ is complete. Indeed, $\overline{B}(x_0,\delta)$ is a closed subset of the complete space $\mathbb{R}^n$, and is therefore complete. Since $J$ is compact, a Cauchy sequence in $E$ for $d_\infty$ converges uniformly to a continuous function whose values still belong to $\overline{B}(x_0,\delta)$.

### The Picard map

For $y\in E$, define

$$
\Phi(y)(t)
=
x_0+
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,y(s))\,ds.
$$

Because $y$ is continuous and $(s,y(s))\in A$ for every $s\in J$, the function $s\mapsto f(s,y(s))$ is continuous. Hence $\Phi(y)$ is a continuous function on $J$.

We must still verify that its values remain in $\overline{B}(x_0,\delta)$. For every $t\in J$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert\Phi(y)(t)-x_0\rVert
&=
\left\lVert
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,y(s))\,ds
\right\rVert\\
&\leq
\left|
\int_{t_0}^{t}\lVert f(s,y(s))\rVert\,ds
\right|\\
&\leq
M|t-t_0|\\
&\leq
MT\\
&\leq
\frac{\delta}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore $\Phi(y)\in E$, and consequently

$$
\Phi:E\longrightarrow E.
$$

### The Picard map is contracting

Let $y,z\in E$. For every $t\in J$, the Lipschitz hypothesis on $f$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert\Phi(y)(t)-\Phi(z)(t)\rVert
&=
\left\lVert
\int_{t_0}^{t}
\bigl(f(s,y(s))-f(s,z(s))\bigr)\,ds
\right\rVert\\
&\leq
C\left|
\int_{t_0}^{t}\lVert y(s)-z(s)\rVert\,ds
\right|\\
&\leq
CT\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty\\
&\leq
\frac{1}{2}\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the supremum over $t\in J$, we obtain

$$
\lVert\Phi(y)-\Phi(z)\rVert_\infty
\leq
\frac{1}{2}\lVert y-z\rVert_\infty.
$$

Thus $\Phi$ is a contracting map on the nonempty complete metric space $(E,d_\infty)$.

### Conclusion of the proof

Banach’s fixed-point theorem gives a unique $x\in E$ such that

$$
\Phi(x)=x.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
x(t)
=
x_0+
\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\,ds
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in J.
$$

By the equivalence between the differential and integral formulations established earlier, this fixed point belongs to $C^1(J;\mathbb{R}^n)$ and is a solution of the Cauchy problem. The uniqueness of Banach’s fixed point gives uniqueness among the solutions whose graphs remain in $A$ on $J$.

The proof is now complete: the bounds on $f$ determine a time interval on which $\Phi$ is well defined and contracting, while the completeness of $E$ ensures that the method of successive approximations converges to the solution.

## The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem for a $C^1$ vector field

The preceding theorem is precise, but its hypotheses involve constants $M$ and $C$ that must be verified on a neighborhood of the initial condition. In most applications, a simpler regularity assumption is sufficient.

**Corollary.** Let $I\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ and $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be open, and let

$$
f:I\times U\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^n
$$

be of class $C^1$. For every $(t_0,x_0)\in I\times U$, there exist $T>0$ and a solution

$$
x:[t_0-T,t_0+T]\longrightarrow U
$$

of

$$
x'(t)=f(t,x(t)),
\qquad
x(t_0)=x_0.
$$

Moreover, this solution is locally unique: if $\widetilde J\subseteq[t_0-T,t_0+T]$ is an interval containing $t_0$ and $\widetilde x:\widetilde J\to U$ is another solution with $\widetilde x(t_0)=x_0$, then

$$
\widetilde x(t)=x(t)
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in\widetilde J.
$$

### Proof of existence

Since $I\times U$ is open and $(t_0,x_0)\in I\times U$, there exists $\delta>0$ such that

$$
A=
\left\{
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n:
|t-t_0|\leq\delta,
\ \lVert x-x_0\rVert\leq\delta
\right\}
$$

is contained in $I\times U$. The set $A$ is compact.

Because $f$ is continuous, it is bounded on $A$. Thus there exists $M>0$ such that

$$
\lVert f(t,x)\rVert\leq M
\qquad
\text{for every }(t,x)\in A.
$$

It remains to obtain a Lipschitz constant with respect to the second variable. Let

$$
D_xf(t,x)
$$

denote the Jacobian matrix of $f$ with respect to $x$. Since $f$ is of class $C^1$, the map $(t,x)\mapsto D_xf(t,x)$ is continuous. It is therefore bounded on the compact set $A$: there exists $C>0$ such that

$$
\lVert D_xf(t,x)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
\leq C
\qquad
\text{for every }(t,x)\in A.
$$

For a fixed $t$, the closed ball $\overline B(x_0,\delta)$ is convex. Hence, for all $(t,x),(t,y)\in A$, the mean value inequality gives

$$
\lVert f(t,x)-f(t,y)\rVert
\leq
\left(
\sup_{z\in\overline B(x_0,\delta)}
\lVert D_xf(t,z)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
\right)
\lVert x-y\rVert
\leq
C\lVert x-y\rVert.
$$

The hypotheses of the precise local theorem are therefore satisfied. With

$$
T=
\min\left\{
\delta,
\frac{\delta}{2M},
\frac{1}{2C}
\right\},
$$

that theorem gives a solution on $[t_0-T,t_0+T]$.

### Why the uniqueness statement is stronger

The fixed-point argument initially proves uniqueness only among functions in

$$
E=C\bigl([t_0-T,t_0+T];\overline B(x_0,\delta)\bigr).
$$

The corollary claims more: any other solution defined on a smaller interval containing $t_0$ must coincide with $x$, without assuming beforehand that all its values remain in the closed ball. This follows from continuity and a continuation argument.

We prove the assertion to the right of $t_0$; the proof to the left is identical. It is enough to fix an arbitrary closed interval

$$
[a,b]\subseteq\widetilde J
$$

with $a\leq t_0\leq b$, and define

$$
\gamma=
\sup\left\{
t\in[t_0,b]:
\widetilde x(s)\in\overline B(x_0,\delta)
\text{ for every }s\in[t_0,t]
\right\}.
$$

Since $\widetilde x(t_0)=x_0$ and $\widetilde x$ is continuous, $\gamma$ is well defined and $\gamma>t_0$. On $[t_0,\gamma]$, both $x$ and $\widetilde x$ satisfy the same integral equation and take their values in $\overline B(x_0,\delta)$. The fixed-point uniqueness argument, applied on this shorter interval, gives

$$
\widetilde x(t)=x(t)
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in[t_0,\gamma].
$$

The construction of $x$ gives the sharper bound

$$
\lVert x(t)-x_0\rVert
\leq
\frac{\delta}{2}
\qquad
\text{for every }t\in[t_0-T,t_0+T].
$$

Consequently,

$$
\lVert\widetilde x(\gamma)-x_0\rVert
\leq
\frac{\delta}{2}.
$$

If $\gamma<b$, continuity of $\widetilde x$ would imply that $\widetilde x(t)$ remains in $\overline B(x_0,\delta)$ for times slightly larger than $\gamma$, contradicting the definition of $\gamma$. Hence $\gamma=b$, and the two solutions coincide on $[t_0,b]$. The same argument shows that they coincide on $[a,t_0]$. Since $[a,b]\subseteq\widetilde J$ was arbitrary, they coincide on all of $\widetilde J$.

This is the usual local form of the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem: a $C^1$ vector field determines one and only one solution through every initial condition, at least on a sufficiently small interval around the initial time.
